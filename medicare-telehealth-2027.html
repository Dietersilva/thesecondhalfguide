Medicare telehealth: covered at home through the end of 2027

The pandemic-era telehealth rules were extended again in February. What they allow, what a visit costs, and what comes back on 1 January 2028 if Congress does nothing.

By Edward Silva · 5 minute read · Checked against reporting on the 2026 appropriations law and independent explainers

This is not new this week. Congress extended Medicare’s expanded telehealth coverage on 3 February 2026, nearly nine months ago, after the previous extension ran out at the end of January. It is worth a page now because Annual Enrollment is running, because the date is easy to get wrong, and because the question it answers is a practical one: can a Medicare patient see a doctor from home?

For now, yes. The extension runs through 31 December 2027.

What the extension keeps in place

Before 2020, Original Medicare paid for telehealth only in narrow circumstances. The patient generally had to live in a rural area and had to be at a medical facility, such as a clinic, when the visit happened. The pandemic rules lifted both limits, and Congress has extended them in stages ever since.

Through the end of 2027, coverage continues to include visits from the patient’s home, without a rural-area requirement. It continues to allow audio-only visits for some services, which matters to people who do not have a smartphone or a reliable video connection. The category of practitioners who can bill for telehealth stays wider than it was before the pandemic, and now includes physical therapists, occupational therapists, speech-language pathologists and audiologists. Not every service qualifies for every format, and audio-only in particular is limited. A patient’s own provider is the one who knows which of its visits are covered.

The extension is a date, not a permanent change. The rule that applies is the rule in force on the day of the visit.

How it got here

The expanded rules began in 2020 as an emergency measure. Congress extended them several times, and they were set to expire on 30 January 2026. They lapsed briefly, then returned in the appropriations law signed on 3 February, which pushed the end date out to the close of 2027. Each extension has been written as a deadline instead of a permanent rule, which is why a patient who used telehealth in 2024 and 2025 can reasonably wonder whether it still works. It does, until the end of next year.

What a visit costs

Telehealth is paid under Part B, so the usual Part B cost-sharing applies: the annual deductible, then 20% of the Medicare-approved amount. The deductible for 2026 is $283. The 2027 amount is announced each fall, and as of 30 October it has not been. The projected figures are discussed on the page on 2027 Medicare costs. A Medigap policy that pays the 20% coinsurance pays it for telehealth as well, because it is the same Part B service.

Nothing about this changes with the extension. It extended where and how a visit can happen, not what Medicare pays for it.

What still limits a visit

The extension lifts location limits. It does not make every visit a telehealth visit. Medicare keeps a list of the services that can be delivered by telehealth, and a provider has to be one that Medicare allows to bill for them. A visit that needs a physical examination, a procedure or a test cannot be done through a screen, and the provider decides which of its appointments are offered that way.

Because the visits are paid under Part B, a patient needs Part B for Original Medicare to pay for them. And anyone who reads that the telehealth rules “expire in January” is reading coverage from before the February law, when that was true. The January date in those articles has passed. The date that matters now is the end of 2027.

What happens on 1 January 2028

Unless Congress extends the rules again or makes them permanent, the pre-pandemic limits come back: the rural and facility restrictions and the narrower list of covered situations. That is about fourteen months away. Congress has extended these rules repeatedly, sometimes after a lapse. That is a pattern, and it is not a promise. Reporting on the extension notes that there has been little movement on bills to make the flexibilities permanent.

So the practical shape of the rule is a window. A person planning care across the end of 2027, for example a recurring series of visits, should expect the rules for visits in January 2028 to be whatever Congress has decided by then.

A note about what this page leaves out

Mental health care by telehealth has its own set of rules, and reporting on them is not consistent about which parts are permanent and which are on the same 2027 clock, so this page does not describe them. The same goes for prescribing of controlled substances by telehealth, which is governed by federal drug enforcement rules rather than by Medicare. Neither is covered here, and the provider’s office is the right place to ask about both.

Medicare Advantage plans are separate again. Each plan sets its own network and visit rules, which is one more reason the plan’s own materials are the ones that apply. Other places where the plan’s own rules matter are on the Advantage or Original page.

What’s worth keeping in mind

  • Medicare’s expanded telehealth coverage runs through 31 December 2027, under a law signed 3 February 2026.
  • A visit can take place from home, without a rural-area requirement.
  • It is paid like any Part B service: the deductible, then 20%. The 2026 deductible is $283; the 2027 figure has not been announced.
  • Audio-only visits are allowed for some services, not all.
  • On 1 January 2028 the older limits return unless Congress acts again.

The rule that governs a visit is the rule on the day of the visit. For the next fourteen months, that rule is generous. After that, it depends on a vote.
