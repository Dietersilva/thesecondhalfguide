About

Why this site exists

Smart, practical information for people roughly 55–70 navigating the financial and lifestyle transition into retirement. Most writing aimed at this stage falls into one of two traps: it talks down to you, or it's a sales pitch wearing a cardigan. We're trying to do something plainer.

The Second Half Guide is a small, independent site about the practical and the personal side of the years after 55 — Medicare, Social Security, estate planning, fraud protection, aging in place, travel, family. Some of it is paperwork. Some of it isn't. We think both belong in the same place, because that's how life actually arrives.

The premise is simple: this stage of life involves a handful of genuinely consequential decisions, most of them explained badly by the people who benefit from your confusion. A Medicare enrollment window is not complicated. It's just described in language designed by a committee. Someone should write it down in normal words.

What we promise

  • Plain language, always. If a sentence needs a second reading, it's our fault, not yours. We name the jargon once and then stop using it.
  • We'll show our sources. Numbers about Social Security, Medicare, or fraud losses come from the agencies that publish them — SSA, CMS, the FTC, the FBI — and we say which, so you can check us.
  • We'll tell you when we don't know. Where an answer genuinely depends on your situation, we'll say so and point you to the right kind of professional rather than faking precision.
  • No fear-selling. Plenty of sites in this space use anxiety to move insurance products. We'd rather you leave a page calmer than you arrived.
  • Fiction is labeled as fiction. We publish short stories alongside the practical guides. They're always marked, at the top and the bottom. We won't dress up an invented story as somebody's real life.

How we're paid

This site is free to read and supported by advertising. That arrangement only works if it stays honest, so a few commitments about it:

  • Ads are clearly marked as advertisements and kept out of the middle of a sentence you're trying to read.
  • Advertisers have no say in what we write. Nobody buys a mention, a recommendation, or a change to an article.
  • If we ever earn a commission when you buy something through a link, we'll say so on that page, in plain sight, before the link.

How we handle accuracy

Rules change — benefit amounts adjust annually, enrollment ages shift with legislation, scam tactics evolve constantly. Every article carries the reporting year of the figures in it. When the underlying numbers change, we update the article and the date rather than quietly leaving stale advice in place.

Here is how a correction actually works, so you know what to expect. Every figure is checked against the sources listed at the foot of that article before it is published, and those sources are named so you can check them yourself rather than take our word for it. Where a number cannot be confirmed, we leave it out instead of publishing our best guess — there are places on this site where a figure is deliberately missing for that reason.

When something is wrong anyway, we fix the article itself rather than quietly editing around it, and we move the checked date only for what was genuinely re-verified.

If you find something wrong, tell us. Corrections are one of the more useful things a reader can send, and we'd rather be corrected than trusted for the wrong reasons.

How this site is made

The research and drafting here are done with AI assistance — Claude for the writing and the first round of verification, and an independent pass by ChatGPT to check the figures against a second opinion. Neither one is treated as the last word. They disagree often enough that the disagreement is the useful part: where they conflict, the number gets checked again, and if it still can't be settled it doesn't get published.

What that process is pointed at is ordinary reporting — the dollar amounts, dates and thresholds that the Social Security Administration, Medicare, the IRS, the CDC and the FBI publish for the current year. Those sources are named at the foot of every article, and they are the thing to trust. This site's job is to find them, put them in one place, and say plainly what they mean.

Edward Silva is the publisher. He decides what gets covered and what doesn't, and he is responsible for everything that appears here.

We're a publisher, not an advisor. Nothing here is personalized financial, legal, medical, or tax advice, and reading it doesn't create a professional relationship. For decisions that turn on your specific numbers, talk to a licensed professional — ideally bringing our articles as a list of questions to ask.

Who writes this

The Second Half Guide is written and edited by Edward Silva.

I spent more than forty years as a computer professional. That career leaves you with one habit that turns out to be useful here: when somebody hands you a summary, you go and read the actual documentation. Forty years of release notes, specifications and vendor claims will teach anyone to distrust a confident paraphrase.

I started this site after hitting the age where the mail changes. Medicare postcards, benefit estimates, insurance pitches dressed up as government notices. When I went looking for plain answers, I mostly found two things: writing that talked to me like I'd lost a step, and writing that was really an advertisement. The straightforward facts — the dates, the thresholds, the dollar figures, the deadline you genuinely cannot miss — were somehow the hardest part to find.

So I write them down. I read the primary source, I link to it, and I put the date on it so you know how fresh it is.

I'm married, with two grown sons, both married themselves — which is its own education in the family side of this stage of life, and a fair amount of why the site isn't only about paperwork.

What I am not: a financial adviser, an attorney, an accountant or an insurance agent. I hold no license and I sell no products. That's a limitation and I'd rather state it plainly than imply otherwise — this site tells you what the rules say and where to check them. It does not tell you what to do with your money.

If something here is wrong, unclear, or has gone out of date, write and tell me. Corrections are among the more useful things a reader can send, and I'd rather be corrected than trusted for the wrong reasons.
