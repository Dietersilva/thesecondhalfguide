The Social Security “COLA activation” scam

The 3.6% increase is automatic. Every year, scammers invent a step — a fee, a phone call, a form — that Social Security’s inspector general says does not exist.

By Edward Silva · 5 minute read · Checked against SSA and SSA Inspector General materials and AARP reporting

On 14 October the Social Security Administration announced a 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment for 2027. The increase is applied automatically. Nobody has to apply, pay or call to receive it. That plain fact is the entire basis of a scam that reappears every fall, and it is easier to see once the real process is laid out beside the fake one.

One note on timing, because the site’s rule is to say how old a fact is. This piece was first checked on 16 October 2026 and updated on 26 October. The Inspector General’s earlier alerts cited below date from 2022, 2024 and 2025. A warning about the 2027 increase itself followed the announcement: reports dated 21–22 October say Social Security is warning that scammers are asking for activation fees, requests to change direct-deposit details, personal or financial information, and visits to imitation websites or downloads of malicious software, all tied to the new increase. The pitch is old and the number in it changes.

The real process, and the fake one
3.6%The 2027 COLA, announced 14 October 2026. It takes effect with January 2027 benefits.
$0The cost of receiving it. There is no fee, no application and no “activation.”

What the scam looks like

The variants differ in packaging. The pitch underneath is always the same: the raise is real, but something has to be done to get it.

The Inspector General’s office has warned about letters made to look like Social Security letterhead that tell the recipient to call a toll-free number to turn on a benefit increase. The number reaches a scammer. It has also warned about calls and messages that demand a fee or personal details to “activate” the increase. AARP’s reporting on the scheme describes messages that ask for $100, and links to websites that closely imitate the agency’s own. In June 2024 the office put out a separate alert that a claimed “$600 June increase” was bogus.

The Inspector General’s position across these alerts is consistent: the annual increase is automatic and needs no extra information or action from the beneficiary.

If someone says there is a step required to receive the COLA, the step is the scam. The raise arrives without one.

Why it works in October

It works because the first half of the story is true. The raise exists, the percentage is in the news, and many beneficiaries are expecting a notice. A letter or call that mentions the real figure sounds informed. In the same weeks, Medicare’s Annual Enrollment has opened and Medicare premiums are about to be announced, so a reader is already braced for mail and calls about money. The mail that is real, such as the plan notices covered on the Annual Notice of Change page, looks a lot like the mail that isn’t.

Impersonators also lean on the agency’s real branding and the names of real officials. A page that looks right is not evidence that it is.

The pitch is old; only the number changes

This is not a one-year problem. AARP was describing COLA-themed Social Security phone scams in 2022. The Inspector General’s office warned in October 2022 that offers to increase a benefit come from criminals, in June 2024 about a “$600 June increase” that did not exist, and in July 2025 about the letters. Each wave borrows whatever the current figure is. For 2026 the figure was 2.8%; for 2027 it is 3.6%. A message that cites the right percentage has told you only that the sender reads the news.

One practical consequence follows from the agency’s own schedule. The real notice does not begin appearing online until late November and does not arrive by mail until December. A letter or call about “your COLA” that shows up in October is ahead of that schedule. That is an inference from the dates rather than a rule the agency has published, and it is a reason to verify the contact independently, not proof that it is fraudulent.

How the real notice reaches you

Social Security sends each beneficiary a notice of the new amount. People with a my Social Security account can read it in the Message Center starting in late November, before the paper copy. People without online notices get a one-page mailed notice in December that lays out the new benefit amount and any deductions, including the Medicare premium. The first higher payment arrives with the January 2027 benefit, on the usual schedule; for most people that means the Wednesday tied to their birthday.

None of it asks for a payment, a password or a Social Security number. If a contact does ask for one, it did not come through that process.

Where the real information lives

The Message Center in a personal my Social Security account, the one-page notice in the mail, and the 3.6% announcement on ssa.gov are the main places. The agency’s own offices and its published phone number, found on ssa.gov rather than on a letter, are also legitimate ways to ask. A text, a social media post, or a call or letter that points to a different website is a reason to stop and check. The practical test is to reach the same information through a route you chose, by typing the agency’s address yourself, instead of the route the message offered you.

What the agency has said about how it contacts people

The Inspector General has said Social Security does not pressure people into acting right away, and advises hanging up or ignoring a suspicious call, text, email or letter, then checking ssa.gov directly instead of through anything in the message. That is the same pattern covered in the page on why scammers need you to act right now, and the follow-through for people locked out of their own account is on the Social Security login page.

What’s worth keeping in mind

  • The 2027 COLA of 3.6% is applied automatically. There is no fee, application or “activation.”
  • A real COLA notice shows the new amount and deductions. It does not ask for money, a password or a Social Security number.
  • Notices appear in the my Social Security Message Center starting in late November, and by mail in December for people who do not use online notices.
  • A toll-free number printed on a letter proves nothing. Letters can be copied from real letterhead; the Inspector General has warned about exactly this.
  • Social Security impersonation can be reported at oig.ssa.gov, and any fraud at reportfraud.ftc.gov.

The scam survives because the premise is true: the raise is real, and people want to be sure they get it. The reassuring fact is the same every year. They will, and nothing about the process requires them to prove anything to anyone first.

This is general fraud-prevention information, not a substitute for reporting an active scam. The official reporting and assistance resources linked above are the right next step if you or someone you know may be a target.
