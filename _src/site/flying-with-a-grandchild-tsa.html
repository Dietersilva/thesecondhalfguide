Flying with a grandchild: what TSA asks of the child, and of you

For a domestic flight, TSA does not require children under 18 to show ID. Adults must. Airlines may have their own rules.

By Edward Silva · 6 minute read · Domestic flights only · Checked against TSA’s own pages

On this page

  3. Formula, drinks and food for a young grandchild
  4. Where TSA sends questions
  5. The part nobody mentions

The most common question before a first flight with a grandchild is about paperwork. For a domestic trip, TSA’s answer is short: the child does not need identification, and you do. That is the TSA checkpoint rule for domestic travel. Airlines may have their own requirements.

On its identification page, TSA states: “TSA does not require children under 18 to provide identification when traveling within the United States.” It adds that unaccompanied minors who are eligible for TSA PreCheck must show an acceptable ID to receive expedited screening, and it tells travelers to contact the airline with questions about its own ID requirements for people under 18. The same page says adult passengers 18 and older must show valid identification at the checkpoint in order to travel.

That second sentence applies to every adult, grandparent or not, whether or not a child is with them.

The line between 12 and 13

TSA says it has developed modified screening procedures for children who appear to be 12 years old and younger, and that standard procedures apply to children 13 and older. The word is “appear.” TSA’s public page does not spell out how officers resolve every borderline case.

What happens to a child at the checkpoint

  • Walking. Children who can walk through the metal detector may go through separately from the adult. Infants and small children may be carried through it.
  • The body scanner. TSA says that if a child can remain standing in the required position for 5 seconds, the child may be screened through advanced imaging technology. It also says an adult who is carrying an infant or child may not be screened by that technology.
  • An alarm. A child 12 or under who alarms has a chance to go through again, or the officer may use other procedures to resolve the alarm and reduce the need for a pat-down.
  • Equipment. Strollers, umbrella strollers, baby carriers, car seats, booster seats and backpacks must be screened by X-ray. TSA says to take infants and children out of strollers and car seats and carry them through the metal detector. Equipment that does not fit through the X-ray machine gets a visual or physical inspection.
  • Who the officer talks to. TSA says officers will consult parents or the traveling guardian about the child’s screening, and that children will not be separated from their parent or guardian. The page does not separately define every grandparent’s status. If that is a concern, ask TSA before you travel.

Formula, drinks and food for a young grandchild

The usual rule for carry-on liquids is a quart bag of containers 3.4 oz or smaller. TSA makes an exception for children: formula, breast milk, toddler drinks and baby or toddler food, including puree pouches, may be carried in amounts over 3.4 oz and do not need to fit in the quart bag. You remove them from the carry-on for separate screening and tell the officer. TSA says it may test liquids for explosives or prohibited items. If you do not want the items X-rayed or opened, you notify the officer. The page adds that additional screening procedures may be required to resolve alarms, so declining does not end screening.

Ice packs, freezer packs, gel packs and liquid-filled teethers are allowed in carry-ons. The page gives only the over-3.4-oz wording and sets no maximum quantity, so this article does not supply one.

Where TSA sends questions

TSA’s page tells travelers to call TSA Cares at (855) 787-2227 72 hours before traveling with questions about screening policies, procedures and what to expect at the checkpoint. It also lists AskTSA, which it describes as available 365 days a year, with live assistance from 8 a.m. to 6 p.m. Eastern. Hours can change, so confirm them on TSA’s page before you rely on them. Screening a child is a judgment made by an officer on the day, which is why TSA points people to a person rather than a table.

The part nobody mentions

Your airline’s rules are separate from TSA’s. TSA’s own page sends travelers to the airline for questions about ID requirements for people under 18. TSA does not address whether an airline wants a letter or other authorization from a child’s parents, and nothing on the TSA pages makes one a TSA requirement. Before departure, confirm the airline’s own rules for a child traveling with an adult who is not a parent, and ask the child’s parents what they want you to carry.

Seating together is an airline matter. Whether an airline will seat an adult beside a child at no extra charge is not something TSA’s pages cover, and this article gives no figure for it. Check the airline’s own policy when you book.

One line on TSA’s children page is out of date. The page still says children 12 and under can leave their shoes, light jackets and headwear on. On 8 July 2025 TSA announced that passengers traveling through domestic airports can keep their shoes on at checkpoints, so shoes no longer distinguish children. The same release says other aspects of TSA’s layered security approach still apply, so officers can still ask for additional screening.

International trips are different. Everything above is about domestic flights. For a trip that crosses a border, see the page on skip-generation travel for the paperwork a destination can require. For the adult’s own ID, see the page on the $45 airport ID fee.

What the rule means in practice

  • TSA does not require ID for a child under 18 on a domestic flight. Airlines may have their own ID requirements.
  • An adult 18 or older must show valid identification at the checkpoint.
  • Children who appear 12 or younger get modified screening; 13 and older get standard screening.
  • Formula, breast milk, toddler drinks and baby or toddler food over 3.4 oz are allowed outside the quart bag. Remove them for separate screening and tell the officer.
  • Call TSA Cares at (855) 787-2227 72 hours ahead for questions about screening.

This is what TSA says. Airline policies may differ, and TSA’s pages carry no publication date, so check the current version in the week you fly.
