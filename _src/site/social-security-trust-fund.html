What the 2026 Trustees Report actually says about Social Security

The numbers behind the 2032 and 2034 headlines: which fund runs short, what share of benefits would still be payable, and what the report does not say.

By Edward Silva · 6 minute read · Checked against the Trustees Report as published by SSA and CRS

Every time Social Security makes news, a second number follows it around. This month the 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment was announced, and within hours it was being printed beside a date in the early 2030s and the word “cut.” The two numbers are unrelated. The COLA comes from a formula applied to consumer prices. The date comes from the annual report of the program’s trustees.

That report was released on 9 June 2026, more than four months ago, and it is still the current one. It is not new this week. It is worth setting out plainly because the headlines compress it badly.

The 2026 Trustees Report, in numbers
9 June 2026When the 2026 Trustees Report was released.
Fourth quarter of 2032When the retirement and survivors fund (OASI) is projected to run out of reserves — one quarter earlier than last year’s projection.
78%The share of scheduled OASI benefits that continuing income would cover at that point, if the law is not changed. The remaining 22% is the projected shortfall.
2034When the retirement, survivors and disability funds, treated as one combined fund, are projected to run out of reserves. CRS gives the third quarter of 2034.
83%The share of scheduled benefits the combined funds’ income would cover at that point.
Through 2100The disability fund (DI) is projected to pay full benefits for the whole 75-year period.
4.42%The long-range actuarial deficit, as a share of taxable payroll, for 2026–2100. Last year’s report put it at 3.82% for 2025–2099.

What “runs out” means

The trust funds hold reserves built up when payroll taxes exceeded benefits paid. Depletion is the date those reserves reach zero. It is not the date benefits stop. Payroll taxes keep arriving every pay period, and the report estimates how much of the scheduled benefit that continuing income would cover. For the retirement fund in the fourth quarter of 2032 the answer is 78%.

That percentage is the whole content of the “22% cut” headline. It is a systemwide financing estimate: the share of scheduled benefits that continuing income could support. It is not an enacted 22% cut to every person’s monthly check, and current law does not say exactly how benefits would be paid once reserves are exhausted. The Congressional Research Service has described two possibilities: full benefits paid late, or reduced benefits paid on time. No decision has been made, and the report does not predict what Congress will do.

Depletion is the date the reserves reach zero, not the date benefits stop. The report counts what the continuing income would pay.

Two funds, one headline

Social Security is paid from two legally separate trust funds: Old-Age and Survivors Insurance for retirement and survivor benefits, and Disability Insurance. They are reported separately and also together. The 2032 date belongs to the retirement fund alone. The 2034 date is what results when the two are combined as though they were one, and it assumes the funds are allowed to be treated that way. The disability fund, on its own, is not projected to run short at all within the report’s 75-year window.

Headlines usually pick one date and drop the other. A reader who sees “2032” in one place and “2034” in another is not seeing a contradiction; they are seeing two different funds.

What changed from the 2025 report

The projected date moved earlier by one quarter. The long-range deficit widened, from 3.82% of taxable payroll in last year’s report to 4.42% in this one. Coverage of the report points to the Trustees’ assumptions about lower birth rates and lower net immigration, and to a lower expected level of revenue from income taxes on benefits following the 2025 tax law. Those are the assumptions behind the projection, and they are exactly the kind of inputs that change from one report to the next.

It is also worth holding onto what a projection is. The report works from a set of intermediate assumptions about the economy and population and gives a single central estimate. A different assumption set would move the date. The Trustees publish a new report every year, and the figures in it are revised each time.

Medicare is reported separately

The same Trustees also report on Medicare’s Hospital Insurance fund, which pays for Part A. The 2026 report projects that fund can pay full benefits until the second quarter of 2033, also one quarter earlier than last year, after which income would cover about 89% of scheduled benefits. That fund is separate from Social Security’s, funded differently, and the dates should not be merged into one “the program runs out” sentence.

What the report does not say

It does not say benefits already being paid will be cut on a particular date; it describes what the law would produce without changes. It does not say what legislation will be passed. It does not change anything about the cost-of-living adjustment, which is set separately by the consumer-price formula covered on the 2027 COLA page. And it does not describe any individual’s benefit: 78% is the aggregate share of scheduled benefits that projected continuing income could support, not a calculation for any one person’s record.

For the ages this site is written for, the dates are close enough to be worth knowing and far enough away that they are not a deadline. A person who is 66 now is 72 in 2032. A person who is 60 is 66. The report’s figures apply to people of every age who are receiving benefits at the time, which is why the same headline shows up in coverage aimed at people of every age.

What’s worth keeping straight

  • 2032 is the retirement and survivors fund alone. 2034 is the combined retirement, survivors and disability funds.
  • 78% and 83% are the shares of scheduled benefits that continuing income would cover at depletion, under current law. They are not announced cuts.
  • The disability fund is not projected to run out within the 75-year projection period.
  • Medicare’s Hospital Insurance fund is projected separately: the second quarter of 2033, with income covering about 89% at that point.
  • The report was released on 9 June 2026. The 3.6% COLA announced in October is unrelated to it.

A headline can hold one number. A report holds several, each with a condition attached. The useful habit is to ask of any date: which fund, what share of benefits, under what assumptions. The 2026 report answers all three, and answers them differently from the way most coverage does.
