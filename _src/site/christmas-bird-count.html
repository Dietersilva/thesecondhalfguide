The Christmas Bird Count: how to join from your backyard

The count began in 1900 as an alternative to a holiday shooting contest. It runs every winter from 14 December to 5 January, it is free, and a feeder count can be done from your own yard.

By Edward Silva · 6 minute read · Checked against Audubon’s own pages and its feeder-watching guidance

On Christmas Day in 1900, the ornithologist Frank Chapman proposed an alternative to a holiday tradition called the Side Hunt, in which participants competed to see who could shoot the most birds and small mammals. Instead of shooting them, go out and count them. That first year, according to Audubon, 27 birders conducted 25 counts in the United States and Canada and tallied around 90 species.

The count has run every winter since. This season it takes place between 14 December 2026 and 5 January 2027. Participation is free, and Audubon says it is open to bird enthusiasts of any level.

How a count is organized

It is not one event. It is more than 2,700 local counts, each inside a designated circle on the map that Audubon says is 15 miles wide, and each held on one day inside the window. A local organizer called a compiler picks the day. Audubon’s pages do not list the 2026–27 dates; your circle’s compiler sets its own.

To take part you register and coordinate in advance with that local leader. Audubon’s Circle Finder locates counts near you. Contact the compiler of a nearby circle to confirm whether your own address can be included in a feeder count.

Two ways to take part

A field count means surveying an area during daylight, alone or in a group. Some counts also include night surveys.

A feeder count is the one that can be done from home. Audubon’s wording is that you count “all the birds you see or hear from your own backyard or local park at a stationary bird feeder within a CBC circle,” and that no binoculars or hiking are required. Two conditions follow. The feeder has to be inside a circle, and all of your counting has to fall within the 24-hour period that is your circle’s count day. Feeder counters were 10,993 of the 85,222 participants last season, about one in eight, by the site’s own arithmetic.

The counting rule that surprises people

Audubon’s feeder-watching guidance asks for the largest number of each species you see or hear at the same time, not a running total. Its example: if the first time you look you see one Northern Cardinal and two Blue Jays, and you hear one chickadee, you write down those numbers. If later you see two cardinals and three Blue Jays at once, you replace the earlier numbers with the larger ones. The guidance is direct: “Do not add your counts together.”

The reason is that a Blue Jay can visit the feeder twenty times. Added together, one bird becomes twenty. Audubon says the method means you will never report an individual bird more than once, and that it matters most for jays, chickadees and nuthatches, which cache food and make repeated visits.

Three details from the same guidance:

  • Count what is nearby, not only what is on the feeder. If two House Sparrows are on the feeder and six more are waiting in a bush, count all eight. Birds that simply fly over, such as Canada Geese, are not counted.
  • Record only on count day, between midnight and 11:59 p.m. for your circle.
  • Record your effort. Note the number of observers and the time you spent actually watching, to the nearest quarter hour. If you are working in the kitchen and can see the feeder for part of an hour, record only that part. Audubon calls effort data equally important to the species counts.

The guidance also asks you to confirm your location with the compiler or sector leader before count day, so that field counters in the area do not count the same birds.

The part nobody mentions: the data is the point

It is easy to read this as a hobby with a holiday theme. It is a long-running dataset. Audubon says four types of data are collected during every count: dates and times, observer effort, weather, and the bird observations. Effort and weather are what let scientists compare a snowy afternoon with two observers to a clear day with ten.

Audubon reports that more than 300 peer-reviewed scientific papers have been published using the data, and says it helped reveal dramatic declines in grassland birds such as the Eastern Meadowlark and the Bobolink. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service describes it the same way: the data is compiled and used to learn about long-term bird trends, and has appeared in reports such as the State of the Birds Report.

That explains why the rules are strict. Audubon’s feeder guidance gives the reason in a sentence: for the data to be scientifically valid, participants must follow the same counting procedure for all species. Even when the male and the female of a species look different and appear at different times, they are counted as two only if seen together.

A small difference between two official histories

Audubon describes 27 birders conducting 25 counts. The Fish and Wildlife Service describes the first count as taking place in 25 locations, from California to Canada. Both report about 90 species. The two accounts do not spell out how the observer count and the location count relate, so this article gives each as its source states it.

What the rule means in practice

  • The window is 14 December 2026 to 5 January 2027. Each circle counts on one day inside it, set by its compiler.
  • Registration and advance coordination with a local leader are required. Audubon’s Circle Finder shows counts near you.
  • A feeder count must be inside a circle and within the circle’s 24-hour count day.
  • Record the largest number seen or heard at the same time for each species. Do not add visits together. Record the time you spend watching and the number of observers.
  • Audubon’s pages do not say whether a compiler can decline a particular feeder, so this article does not.

For readers who would like to try it, the first step is the Circle Finder and a call or message to the nearest circle’s compiler. Audubon lists cbcadmin@audubon.org for general support. Researchers who plan to use the data in published work are asked to notify Audubon through its online data-use form.
