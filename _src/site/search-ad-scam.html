When a Medicare search result is an ad

The FTC warns that scammers can buy search ads that imitate official health-insurance sites. Check the website’s actual address and use verified help.

By Edward Silva · 5 minute read · Checked against the FTC’s September 2026 consumer alert and independent reporting that cites it

Most open-enrollment scam warnings are about the phone. The Federal Trade Commission’s alert, published on September 28 ahead of Medicare and Marketplace open enrollment, is about something quieter: the search results page. Its point is simple. Scammers can pay to appear at the top of search results, and a paid result can look a great deal like the real thing.

Someone searching for “Medicare plans” during open enrollment is who such an ad is built for. The link can go to a page that borrows official-looking logos and keywords, or to a number that rings a call center. The FTC says a person who uses that link or number can end up paying for something that is not health insurance, or exposing personal information to medical identity theft.

What the FTC alert says, at a glance
Where it startsA paid ad at the top of search results, not a call or a letter.
What it imitatesMedicare.gov and HealthCare.gov, using similar web addresses, official-looking logos and the keywords you searched for.
What it can cost youPayment for a plan that is not health insurance, or medical identity theft.
Official addressesMedicare.gov for Medicare and HealthCare.gov for federal Marketplace information. Check the hostname that actually loads.
Official phone1-800-633-4227 (1-800-MEDICARE) for Medicare.
Medicare open enrollment15 October – 7 December, here for 2027 coverage.
Where to reportReportFraud.ftc.gov and your state attorney general; Medicare-related scams also to Medicare at the number above.

What a paid result looks like

For the official Medicare website, type https://www.medicare.gov into the browser’s address bar. Then check the hostname that actually loads. Medicare.gov and its genuine subdomains are different from a lookalike such as medicare.gov.example.com, where the real domain is the part just before the final “.com”. Only verified U.S. government organizations can register .gov domains, so a true .gov hostname is a useful signal, but check the hostname itself, not the page title or a string that merely contains “.gov.” For federal Marketplace information, use HealthCare.gov.

If you do search, a search engine labels paid placements, usually with a small “Ad” or “Sponsored” tag near the result. The tag is easy to miss.

Not every ad is a scam. Plenty of paid results come from legitimate private businesses, such as brokers and insurers. The point is narrower: the official Plan Finder is free, and browsing plans and enrolling in one are different stages. Be cautious about giving a Medicare number to anyone who reached you first or to a lead form on a page you cannot verify. A provider, an insurer or a trusted Medicare counselor you chose may legitimately need information for other purposes.

An Ad or Sponsored label means someone paid for placement. It does not establish who runs the destination or whether it is trustworthy.

Why the ad works

An ad does not have to be a convincing forgery. It only has to be there at the moment a person is looking, with a headline that echoes the search, and a logo or a web address close enough to pass at a glance. Search engines carry the ads because advertisers pay for the position, and the FTC’s alert treats that as a fact about how the system works, not a defect in the reader.

It is not a new concern for the agency. FTC staff sent warning letters to 21 healthcare plan marketers and lead generators in December 2024. On October 29, 2026, FTC staff announced warning letters to 25 healthcare-plan marketers and lead generators about potentially deceptive marketing. Neither set of letters alleges wrongdoing by the recipients. The newer letters concern Marketplace insurance and other healthcare products, so they are broader healthcare-marketing warnings, not proof of a new Medicare paid-search impersonation case. The FTC also held a public roundtable on healthcare scams around open enrollment on September 29.

Why open enrollment

Medicare open enrollment runs from 15 October to 7 December, and Marketplace open enrollment follows. People shopping for coverage are searching for exactly the terms these ads target, so the timing matters to the scheme: the ad is waiting when a person who is already looking types in the query.

It also pairs with the phone problem. A web form that collects a name and number is how a call center can get the list it dials later. The sensible response is the same as for a cold call: be careful with a Medicare number, Social Security number or bank details, and give them only to a company or agent you chose, reached through an address or number you verified yourself. The older piece on why the phone rings more in October covers the calling side.

If you already clicked

Clicking does not automatically mean your identity was stolen. Close a suspicious page. If a link downloaded software or you installed something, follow the FTC’s malware guidance. If you disclosed information, the next steps depend on what you shared.

If you paid, contact the bank, card issuer or payment service promptly and ask about reversing the payment. If your Medicare number may have been misused, contact 1-800-MEDICARE and review your claims for services you did not receive. Use IdentityTheft.gov for recovery guidance after identity misuse; the FTC also provides protective steps when a Social Security number was exposed but misuse is uncertain. Report the scam at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and to your state attorney general.

Help is available. State Health Insurance Assistance Programs (SHIPs) offer Medicare coverage counseling and plan-comparison help. Senior Medicare Patrol (SMP) helps people prevent, detect and report suspected Medicare fraud. Some organizations run both, but the two roles are distinct.

What to check

  • Go to Medicare.gov by typing https://www.medicare.gov, or by using a bookmark made once from a source you trust.
  • Check the hostname that actually loads, not the page title or a string that merely contains “.gov.”
  • An Ad or Sponsored label means paid placement, nothing more.
  • Be wary of a page that asks for payment, or for a Medicare number, before it shows you plans, and of anyone who contacts you first. The official Plan Finder is free.
  • If you were taken in: contact your bank or card issuer if you paid, call Medicare at 1-800-633-4227 if Medicare is involved, and report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

The first search result is not always the official one. Checking the actual address and using a verified contact reduces the chance of handing information to an impersonator.

This is general fraud-prevention information, not a substitute for reporting an active scam. The official reporting and assistance resources linked above are the right next step if you or someone you know may be a target.
