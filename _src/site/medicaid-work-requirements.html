Medicaid work requirements start 1 January 2027: what the rule says for ages 55 to 64

Adults 19 to 64 covered through Medicaid expansion will have to show 80 hours a month of work, study or service, or an exemption. The deadline, what counts, and what happens after a missed month.

By Edward Silva · 6 minute read · Checked against CMS’s rule and independent reporting that cites it

Most coverage of Medicaid work requirements is about younger adults. The age range in the rule is 19 to 64. That includes a lot of readers of this site: people in their late fifties and early sixties who left a job, have no employer coverage, and are covered through their state’s Medicaid expansion while they wait for Medicare at 65.

The requirement is not new this week. CMS issued the rule that tells states how to apply it on 1 June 2026, and it was published in the Federal Register two days later. The date that matters is the one Congress set: states must have it running by 1 January 2027. That is eight weeks away.

The Medicaid work requirement, in numbers
1 January 2027The date states must begin applying the requirement. States may start earlier.
1 June 2026When CMS issued its interim final rule on how states apply it.
Ages 19 to 64The adults covered, primarily people enrolled through Medicaid expansion.
80 hours a monthThe qualifying activity needed: work, community service, a work program, or at least half-time education.
$580 a monthEarning at least this much counts instead of the hours. It is the federal minimum wage times 80 hours.
30 daysThe time a person gets to respond to a notice of noncompliance before coverage ends.
31 December 2028The latest a state could delay, if it receives a good-faith-effort exemption.

Who is covered, and where it stops

The requirement applies to adults in the Medicaid expansion group, the people who qualify because their state extended Medicaid to adults with incomes up to a set level. It does not apply to every person with Medicaid. The age range in the rule ends at 64. A person who turns 65 is outside it, which is a reminder that for many readers the requirement would apply for a few years at most before Medicare begins. How that handoff works is on the Medicare enrollment page.

Medicaid is run by states, so the details of how a month is verified, what paperwork is accepted and when the first notices go out differ from state to state. States are also allowed to begin earlier than January. And a state that cannot be ready can apply for a temporary good-faith-effort exemption, which delays implementation until no later than 31 December 2028. Whether a given state has done so is a question for the state’s Medicaid agency.

What counts toward the 80 hours

The rule counts several kinds of activity: employment, community service, participation in a work program, and education at least half-time. Earning at least $580 a month counts in place of the hours, which is the federal minimum wage of $7.25 multiplied by 80.

Compliance is checked when a person applies, and states may look back over one to three months before the application month to see whether the requirement was met. Between renewals, reporting says the rule gives people some flexibility about which months they show the activity.

The question is not whether you worked this week. It is whether the state can verify 80 hours, or an exemption, for the month it checks.

Who is exempt

The exemptions listed in coverage of the rule include parents or caregivers of a child 13 or younger or of a person with a disability, veterans with a total disability rating, people who are medically frail, former foster youth, and American Indians and Alaska Natives. Residence in a federally identified disaster area is also reported as an exemption by one source. The rule limits how much a person can rely on their own statement to show medical frailty, which means the state may ask for documentation.

There is no exemption based on age alone listed in the coverage reviewed for this page, including for people in their late fifties or early sixties. Anyone who thinks an exemption may apply should read the state’s own list, because the rule leaves states to build the process and the evidence they accept.

What happens when a month is missed

If a state cannot verify that a person met the requirement or qualified for an exemption, it must send a notice and give the person 30 days to show compliance. Coverage continues during those 30 days, and the state has to check whether the person qualifies another way before ending it. Coverage ends only if the window closes without a satisfactory response. The days are calendar days, and the termination can take effect after the window rather than the day after.

Two details were left out of this page because sources disagreed. One is whether a person who loses Medicaid over the requirement can get a Marketplace premium tax credit. Reporting is split, and the answer should come from the CMS rule or a state notice. The other is a reported 90-day window to regain coverage without a new application, which only one guide mentioned. Neither should be assumed.

Where this sits among the other changes

For a reader who is 60 and between jobs, this is one piece of a larger set of coverage dates. The Marketplace side is on the ACA subsidy cliff page, the employer-coverage side is on the COBRA page, and the programs that help once a person has Medicare are on the Medicare Savings Programs page. This page is about the one that turns on a monthly record.

What’s worth keeping in mind

  • States must begin applying the requirement by 1 January 2027; some may start earlier, and a state with a good-faith exemption can delay to the end of 2028.
  • It covers expansion adults 19 to 64: 80 hours a month of work, service, a work program or half-time school, or $580 a month in earnings.
  • Exemptions include caregivers of a child 13 or younger, medically frail people, veterans with a total disability rating, former foster youth and American Indians and Alaska Natives. Age alone is not listed.
  • A missed or unverified month brings a notice and 30 days to respond before coverage ends.
  • The details of verification vary by state. Your state’s Medicaid agency is the place to check yours.

The deadline is set by federal law and the process is set by each state. For anyone covered this way, the practical question is what the state will ask to see, and when it will ask.
