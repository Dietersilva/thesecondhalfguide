Skip-gen travel: grandparents and grandkids, no parents

A specific kind of family trip is growing fast — grandparent and grandchild, without the grandchild’s parents along. The numbers behind it, and the one piece of paperwork that actually matters if the trip crosses a border.

By Edward Silva · 5 minute read · Verified against industry survey and State Department guidance

“Skip-gen” travel is a specific thing, distinct from the family reunion trip where three generations all go together: a grandparent takes a grandchild somewhere, and the child’s parents stay home. It has a name now because it has gotten common enough to have one.

The numbers
How common it isIn the Family Travel Association’s annual US Family Travel Survey, 71% of grandparents say they have taken a multigenerational trip recently, and 57% say they are planning one.
Skip-gen is grandparent-ledOn skip-gen trips specifically — grandchild, no parents — grandparents plan about 75% of them and pay for about 84%.
Who pays on the wider categoryAcross multigenerational trips broadly, roughly half of grandparents pay for the entire trip themselves; most of the rest split costs with their adult children.
Not the same tripA multigenerational trip (everyone travels together) and a skip-gen trip (grandparent and grandchild only) are reported as two distinct, both-growing categories in the same survey data — not one blended trend.

The one piece of paperwork that actually matters

The United States does not require a written permission letter for a minor to travel internationally with a grandparent rather than a parent. Individual destination countries often do, or airlines may ask for one at check-in even where the destination itself does not strictly require it. The State Department’s own guidance is to check with the embassy or consulate of wherever the trip is headed, since the requirement is set by the destination, not by the US.

Where a letter is expected, the standard version is simple: both parents sign a statement naming the grandparent and acknowledging the child is traveling with that person, with their permission, and it is preferably in English and notarized. It costs nothing but a trip to a notary and a few weeks of lead time — the kind of thing that is easy to forget until it is the reason a family is pulled aside at a border.

Nobody plans a skip-gen trip around the paperwork. It is the one part of the trip that can actually stop it, and it is also the cheapest and easiest part to get right in advance.

What to actually do before booking

  • If the trip crosses a border, contact the destination country’s embassy or consulate directly to ask about consent-letter requirements — do not assume US rules are the only ones that apply.
  • Where a letter is expected, both parents should sign it, and getting it notarized is the safer default even when a destination does not explicitly require notarization.
  • Carry the grandchild’s own passport and, if relevant, proof of the family relationship (a birth certificate) — airline staff and border agents ask for this more often than families expect.
  • For a domestic trip, the paperwork bar is much lower, but a signed permission note covering medical treatment authorization is still worth having in case of an urgent care visit.
  • Book with the grandparent as the primary contact wherever the itinerary allows it, since that is who will be asked to produce documentation if anything is questioned.

The trend itself is the easy part — grandparents already know whether they want to take a grandchild somewhere. The paperwork is the part that decides whether the plan survives contact with an actual border.
