WISeR adds a payment-review step to Original Medicare in six states

A CMS pilot that began in January 2026 adds prior authorization or pre-payment review for certain Part B services in Arizona, New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, Texas and Washington. Medicare Advantage is not part of it.

By Edward Silva · 6 minute read · Checked against CMS, CRS, KFF and independent reporting on the model

One common argument for choosing Original Medicare over an Advantage plan is that Original Medicare does not make a provider ask permission first. In six states that has been less true since January 2026. The pilot is called WISeR, short for Wasteful and Inappropriate Service Reduction. It puts a payment-review step on a defined list of Part B services, either approval before the service or review before payment.

It is worth a page now because the choice between Original Medicare and an Advantage plan is made during open enrollment, and one familiar talking point has an exception for people who live in these states.

The WISeR model, in numbers
Six statesArizona, New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, Texas and Washington.
January 2026When the model began. Services provided before 15 January 2026 are not subject to it.
31 December 2031When the model is scheduled to end, per the Congressional Research Service.
Original Medicare onlyMedicare Advantage plans are not part of WISeR.
Selected servicesA defined list of services and codes in CMS’s current operational guide. Sources count the categories differently.
Two routesThe provider either requests prior authorization before the service, or the claim goes through post-service, pre-payment review.

What it is

Prior authorization means a provider gets approval before delivering a service, or Medicare may not pay for it. Advantage plans have used it for years. Original Medicare mostly has not, with exceptions such as some equipment, covered on the equipment prior authorization page. WISeR extends the idea to a set of Part B services. It is a CMS Innovation Center model, which means a time-limited test and not a permanent rule.

The model is keyed to providers and suppliers in the six states. It applies to Original Medicare claims and not to Medicare Advantage members, who are subject to their own plans’ rules. Railroad Medicare is excluded. Whether it will be extended to other states or services has not been announced in the reporting reviewed.

Which services

The list is a set of Part B services chosen as vulnerable to overuse. KFF’s description names skin substitutes used in treating severe or chronic wounds, electrical nerve stimulator implants and certain orthopedic pain services such as cervical fusion and epidural steroid injections. Other summaries include arthroscopy for knee osteoarthritis. The authoritative list is the selected services and codes in CMS’s current operational guide for providers, which CMS can update during the model, so this page does not give a single number.

The rule is not that everything in Original Medicare now needs approval. It is that a specific list is reviewed, for providers in six specific states.

Who decides, and how the participants are paid

Each state has one participating technology company that reviews requests. Reporting says the companies use artificial intelligence to help with review and that licensed clinicians make decisions not to approve a request. Participants are not paid a flat fee. They are paid a share of savings attributed to the review process, and payments are adjusted for timeliness, accuracy and the provider’s experience with the process. CMS has also said inaccurate results can lower a participant’s payment. Critics see an incentive to deny; supporters say the model could reduce spending that falls on taxpayers and beneficiaries.

Providers and suppliers submit the requests, not patients. A patient’s practical contact with the model is often indirect: a scheduled procedure waiting for an approval, or a non-affirmation the provider has to pursue. When a request is not approved, the provider can resubmit it, with a peer-to-peer conversation available, which is different from a formal appeal of a denied claim. CMS’s operational guide is the place to confirm which route applies to a specific service. CMS has also described a process under which providers with high approval rates can be exempted from review.

What is contested

The model has drawn criticism from advocacy groups, medical associations and some members of Congress. A bill to repeal it, H.R. 5940, the Seniors Deserve SMARTER Care Act, was introduced in the House on 7 November 2025 by Representative Suzan DelBene. The reporting reviewed shows no action beyond its referral to committee. The Center for Medicare Advocacy published an article on the model on 26 March 2026 that raised concerns; that is the view of an advocacy group and not a measured finding.

Where this fits in the enrollment choice

None of this is a recommendation. It is one fact that belongs next to the others in the choice covered on the Advantage or Original page: Advantage plans use prior authorization broadly, Original Medicare uses it for a defined list, and in six states that list now carries a payment-review step. The window for changing a plan is covered on the open enrollment page, and the services that fall outside both are on the gaps page.

What’s worth keeping in mind

  • Since January 2026, a pilot called WISeR has put a payment-review step on selected Part B services in Arizona, New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, Texas and Washington.
  • It applies to Original Medicare only; Medicare Advantage plans are outside it. It is scheduled to run to 31 December 2031.
  • The review comes in two forms: prior authorization before the service, or pre-payment review of the claim afterwards.
  • Providers and suppliers submit the requests. The participants are paid a share of savings, which is both the model’s design and the main criticism.
  • The model is a time-limited test. A repeal bill was introduced in November 2025 and has not advanced in the reporting reviewed.

The accurate statement is narrower than either side of the argument makes it. Original Medicare still does not require approval for most services. In six states, for a defined list, there is now a review step before payment.
