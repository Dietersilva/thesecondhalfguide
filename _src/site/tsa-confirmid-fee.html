The $45 airport ID fee: who pays it, and who does not

Since 1 February, a domestic flyer without REAL ID or another accepted ID can pay $45 for TSA’s backup identity check. It is optional, it does not guarantee you board, and it is paid in one place only.

By Edward Silva · 5 minute read · Checked against TSA’s ConfirmID announcements and reporting

Two things happened at American airports in the last eighteen months, and they are easy to blur together. On 7 May 2025 the REAL ID rule began to be fully enforced: a standard driver’s license without the star no longer works as identification at a TSA checkpoint. Then on 1 February 2026 TSA added a fee to the end of that story. This page is about the fee.

It will not touch most readers. TSA’s early numbers, released 5 February, said between 95% and 99% of travelers were already showing a REAL ID or another accepted document. But the people it does touch tend to find out at the worst moment, which is why the details are worth having first.

What ConfirmID is

It is a fallback, not a new requirement. A traveler who arrives at security without a REAL ID or another accepted form of identification can pay the $45 fee in advance on Pay.gov, show the emailed receipt at the checkpoint, and go through an alternative process to verify who they are. TSA has said that process may take longer, and that passengers who do not present an accepted ID should expect increased wait times. The payment covers a 10-day window starting from the first travel date on the receipt. A traveler who passes through another checkpoint after those ten days, still without an accepted ID, would pay again.

Paying does not buy certainty. TSA’s own wording is that the fee does not guarantee successful verification. A traveler whose identity cannot be confirmed can still be turned away at the checkpoint, and the fee is reported to be nonrefundable. For anyone with a flight to catch, that is the sentence that matters.

What is accepted without the fee

A REAL ID-compliant license or identification card, which carries a star marking, is the common one. But a U.S. passport or passport card works without any fee, as do the DHS trusted-traveler cards (Global Entry, NEXUS, SENTRI and FAST), a state-issued Enhanced Driver’s License, and military identification, including cards issued to dependents. TSA keeps the complete list on its identification page, and it is the list to check before a flight, because it can change.

That has a practical consequence for the readers of this site. A passport card, which many people keep for a cruise, is an accepted document for a domestic flight. So is a Global Entry card, one of the DHS trusted-traveler cards TSA lists. The airport security page covers what those programs cost. And a temporary paper license, issued while a new one is in the mail, is not an accepted document on its own, a gap that catches people who renewed shortly before a trip.

The fee is not the cost of flying without ID. It is the cost of asking TSA to try to verify you without it.

Who actually ends up paying it

The groups are predictable. People whose licenses were renewed before the star was added and who never went back to the motor vehicles office. People who have not flown since 2019 and carry an older card. People who have moved states and are carrying a license that was never replaced. And people with a perfectly good license who simply did not realize it lacked the star. None of this is a judgment about the traveler. It is a gap between a renewal date and a federal deadline.

The rule underneath is unchanged and simple: for a domestic flight, an adult generally needs one accepted form of identification, not two. A traveler who arrives without one may be offered the identity-verification option, which is what the fee pays for, rather than being turned away outright. A person who holds a valid passport already has an accepted document, and the fee is irrelevant to them.

Where scammers fit in

A federal fee with a payment page and a deadline is exactly the shape a scam takes. The official path is narrow: TSA says the fee can be paid only through Pay.gov, reached from TSA.gov/ConfirmID, and that the payment is followed by a receipt emailed from Pay.gov. A text, an email or a search ad offering to “process” the TSA fee for a charge is not that path. This is the same pattern covered on the five-minute rule page: an official-looking page, a fee and a reason to hurry.

What TSA has not said

The fee was introduced in a TSA announcement in December 2025 and described by TSA on its own site. The agency has not published, in the material available here, a count of how many travelers have used ConfirmID or how many of them were cleared. The figure it has given is the share of travelers who arrive with a compliant ID, which is the 95% to 99% above. Anything more specific about outcomes would be a guess, and this page does not make one.

Other airport rules that changed recently are on the airport security page, and the help available to travelers who need it is on the airport help page. Passport rules that turn people away at borders, which are a different problem, are on the passport page.

What’s worth keeping in mind

  • Since 1 February 2026, travelers 18 and older without REAL ID or another accepted ID can pay $45 to use TSA ConfirmID. It is optional.
  • The fee is paid only on Pay.gov, through TSA.gov/ConfirmID, and covers 10 days.
  • Paying does not guarantee verification or boarding.
  • A passport, passport card, Global Entry card or Enhanced Driver’s License is accepted without the fee. A temporary paper license is not.
  • Children under 18 do not need ID for domestic flights.

For almost everyone, the answer is to bring something on TSA’s accepted list. The fee exists for the few travelers in a hundred who did not.
