What the FTC’s new report says older adults lost to fraud

About $3 billion in reported losses in 2025, five times the 2020 figure. What the number counts, what it leaves out, and what it says about how the losses happen.

By Edward Silva · 5 minute read · Checked against the FTC’s report and press release and independent coverage

On 19 October the Federal Trade Commission sent Congress its annual report on older consumers, titled Protecting Older Consumers 2025–2026. The headline was a dollar figure that is easy to repeat and easy to misread: people 60 and older reported losing about $3 billion to fraud in 2025.

This is the second thing worth saying about the number. The first is how recent it is. The report is eleven days old as of this writing, and its figures cover calendar 2025. An earlier FTC release, in December 2025, described 2024 data, and the two should not be mixed.

What the $3 billion counts

The figure is reported loss. It is what people told the FTC, or what reached the FTC through the other agencies and organizations that feed its Consumer Sentinel database. A scam that nobody reports is not in it. That is the single most important thing about the number, and it cuts the other way from what most readers assume. The $3 billion is a floor.

The FTC says as much itself. Using different assumptions about how many people never report, it estimates the true cost to older adults in 2025 at somewhere between $13.3 billion and $119.5 billion. A range that wide says the agency does not know the answer. It does say the reported figure is a small piece of it.

Why the total grew fivefold

From about $600 million in 2020 to about $3 billion in 2025 is not a story about more people being fooled by the same thing. According to coverage of the report, the rise was driven largely by individual losses of $100,000 or more, often to investment scams, romance scams and impersonators. A smaller number of very large losses moves a total much more than a large number of small ones.

That is also why investment scams top the list. They reported more than $1 billion in losses for the age group, a 42% increase over the year before. Coverage of the report says the victims were often reached through social media.

The biggest losses are not the clumsy scams. They are the patient ones.

What the report does not say

It does not say older adults are fooled more often than younger ones. What the FTC reports is a difference in the size of the loss: older adults reported much higher median losses than younger adults in 2025. A person can be careful and still be the target of the kind of scam that goes after a savings account, because that is where the money is.

It also does not rank scams by how likely any one person is to meet them. A category with a large total can be rare and costly. A category with a small total can be everywhere and cheap. That distinction matters for the next time one of these headlines appears.

What older adults most often report

Coverage of the report lists the categories in which older adults are more likely than younger adults to report a loss: tech support scams, prize, sweepstakes and lottery scams, romance scams, government impersonation, and scams in which someone impersonates a friend or family member. The list is a mix of the old and the new. A prize notice is a letter-era scam. A cloned voice is not. Both appear in the same table because the report counts what people reported, not the technology used.

Reading the next headline

A fraud number is only as useful as three details attached to it: the year it covers, whether it is a reported or an estimated figure, and who did the counting. The $3 billion is reported, 2025 and the FTC’s. The $119.5 billion is estimated, also 2025 and also the FTC’s, and it is the upper end of a range. Quoting one as if it were the other is the most common way these numbers go wrong in circulation.

Where to report

Reports go to reportfraud.ftc.gov. Social Security impersonation can also be reported to the agency’s Inspector General at oig.ssa.gov. Reporting is how a loss reaches the number at the top of this page, which is part of why the FTC keeps asking for it.

The FTC’s long-standing line on payment is that a legitimate business or agency will not ask for payment by gift card, wire transfer or cryptocurrency. That is a statement about how the requests arrive, not about the people receiving them.

What’s worth keeping in mind

  • The FTC report released 19 October 2026 covers 2025: about $3 billion in reported losses by people 60 and older, up roughly fivefold from 2020.
  • That figure is reported loss only. The FTC’s own estimate of the full cost runs from $13.3 billion to $119.5 billion.
  • Investment scams were the costliest category, with over $1 billion reported, up 42% in a year.
  • The report is about the size of losses, not about who is more easily fooled. Older adults reported much higher median losses than younger adults.
  • Fraud can be reported at reportfraud.ftc.gov.

The number will probably be quoted all year. It is worth knowing what it is a count of before it is.

This is general fraud-prevention information, not a substitute for reporting an active scam. The official reporting and assistance resources linked above are the right next step if you or someone you know may be a target.
