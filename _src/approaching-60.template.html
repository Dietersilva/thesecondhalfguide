Approaching 60 and beyond: what actually changes, and what doesn't

Turning 60 doesn't flip a switch. A few things genuinely do need your attention on a schedule — most things don't change at all unless you let them. Here's how to tell the difference.

By Edward Silva · 7 minute read · Reviewed for accuracy against 2026 program rules

Somewhere around 60, a strange thing starts happening: mail shows up. Medicare postcards. Social Security estimate letters. Ads for hearing aids you don't need yet. It can feel like the world decided, all at once, that you've crossed into a new category of person.

You haven't. But a handful of things do genuinely have deadlines attached to them now, and knowing what they are — and, just as importantly, what doesn't need to change — makes this stretch a lot less noisy.

What actually has a clock on it

Most of what feels urgent about this decade comes down to four things with real dates attached:

  • Medicare eligibility generally starts at 65, with a seven-month enrollment window centered on your birthday month. Miss it without other coverage in place and you can face a permanent late-enrollment penalty — this is the one deadline on this list that's genuinely unforgiving. We cover the enrollment windows and plan choices in our Medicare guide.
  • Social Security can be claimed any time from 62 to 70, and the age you choose changes your monthly payment for the rest of your life. There's real math behind that decision — see below.
  • Required withdrawals from most retirement accounts (401(k)s, traditional IRAs) begin at 73 if you were born between 1951 and 1959, and at 75 if you were born in 1960 or later — which is most people reading this. This one's easy to lose track of if it's still a few years off.
  • Full retirement age for Social Security is 67 if you were born in 1960 or later — not 65, despite what a lot of people still assume. That gap matters when you're doing the claiming math.

The goal isn't to front-load every decision at once. It's to know which ones have real deadlines and which ones you get to take your time on.

What doesn't have to change

The cultural script says 60 is when things start winding down. The actual data on aging doesn't back that up nearly as much as the greeting cards suggest.

  • Your health trajectory is still largely yours to shape. Strength training, cardiovascular fitness, and sleep quality all still respond to effort in your 60s and 70s — the body doesn't stop adapting, it just asks for more consistency.
  • Learning ability doesn't fall off a cliff. Vocabulary and accumulated expertise (what researchers call "crystallized intelligence") typically continue improving well into your 70s.
  • Purpose and identity don't have to be tied to a job title. Plenty of the most engaged, energized people in this stage of life are several years into a second act nobody would have predicted for them at 40.

A short list to actually act on

Not a 40-item retirement checklist — just the handful of things worth doing something about in the next year or two:

  • Request your Social Security statement at ssa.gov and check the earnings record for errors — they happen more often than you'd think, and they quietly shrink your future benefit.
  • If you're within a year of 65, mark your seven-month Medicare enrollment window on a calendar now, not later.
  • If you don't have a will or power-of-attorney documents, this decade — not "someday" — is the right time. It's a shorter task than it sounds like.
  • Have one honest conversation with whoever you'd lean on if your health changed suddenly — about what you'd actually want, not just paperwork.

This is general information, not personal advice. Medicare penalties, retirement account rules, and estate laws vary by your specific situation and state. When money or legal decisions are on the line, this is a good starting point for the questions to ask — not a substitute for someone looking at your actual numbers.
