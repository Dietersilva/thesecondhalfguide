The 2027 Social Security COLA is 3.6%

Social Security announced the official 2027 raise on 14 October: 3.6%, starting with January benefits. The earnings limits and the taxable maximum moved with it — and Medicare’s 2027 premium, which decides what you keep, has not been announced.

By Edward Silva · 5 minute read · Checked against the SSA announcement and independent reports

On 14 October 2026 the Social Security Administration announced a 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment for 2027. It applies to Social Security benefits starting with the January 2027 payments, and to Supplemental Security Income payments starting 31 December 2026. It is the largest adjustment since the 8.7% for 2023, and it is higher than the 2.8% that took effect in January 2026. Nobody has to apply for it; the increase is added automatically.

The official 2027 figures
3.6%The 2027 COLA, announced 14 October 2026. The 2026 COLA was 2.8%.
$2,095 → $2,170The average retired-worker benefit before and after the increase, according to the Social Security Administration — about $75 a month.
$192,300The 2027 taxable maximum — the most earnings subject to Social Security tax. It was $184,500 in 2026.
$25,560The 2027 earnings limit for people who have not reached full retirement age all year. It was $24,480 in 2026.
$67,920The 2027 earnings limit in the year a person reaches full retirement age.
31 December 2026When the increased SSI payment is paid.
Not yet announcedThe 2027 Medicare Part B premium and deductible. CMS had not published them as of 16 October 2026.

How the 3.6% was set

The COLA is not a policy choice or a negotiation. It comes from the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers — CPI-W — averaged across July, August and September, and compared with the same three-month average a year earlier. The percentage change, rounded to the nearest tenth of a percent, becomes the COLA. September’s inflation data, released the same day as the announcement, is the last piece, which is why the number cannot be known before mid-October.

The independent trackers this site cited in September, which were running the same public formula on two of the three months, had put the range at 3.5% to 3.6%. The official result landed at the top of it. That is the ordinary pattern: estimates narrow as the quarter fills in, and the final figure comes from the government’s own calculation, not from any forecaster’s.

3.6% is the percentage. What lands in the account is a different number, and the difference is set by what is deducted before it arrives.

What 3.6% means in dollars

The Social Security Administration puts the average retired-worker benefit at $2,095 before the increase and $2,170 after — roughly $75 a month. That is an average. A COLA is a percentage of the benefit a person already receives, not a flat amount added to every check alike, so the dollar figure scales with the benefit:

A $1,500 benefit rises by about $54 a month. A $2,000 benefit rises by about $72. A $3,000 benefit rises by about $108. The arithmetic is the same for everyone; only the starting point differs.

The raise and the deposit are still different numbers

The Part B premium is usually deducted from Social Security before the payment arrives, and the 2027 premium has not been announced. For comparison, the 2026 premium rose from $185.00 to $202.90 — an increase of $17.90 that came out of the 2.8% COLA before it reached most people’s accounts. A hold-harmless provision limits how much of a COLA the Part B increase can absorb for many people who have the premium withheld from their benefit, but it does not cover everyone: people newly enrolled in Medicare, higher earners paying the IRMAA surcharge, and people whose premium is paid by Medicaid are among those it does not protect. The mechanics are laid out on the COLA page.

Until CMS publishes the 2027 premium and deductible, the net effect of the 3.6% cannot be calculated. Projections exist, but a projection is not an announcement, and this site does not treat one as the other.

The other numbers that moved

The earnings limit. A person who claims benefits before full retirement age and keeps working has benefits withheld once earnings pass a yearly limit. For 2027 that limit is $25,560 for anyone below full retirement age for the entire year: $1 in benefits is withheld for every $2 earned above it. In the calendar year a person reaches full retirement age, the limit is $67,920 and the rate is $1 withheld for every $3 above it, counting only the earnings before the month full retirement age is reached. After that month there is no limit. Benefits withheld are not forfeited: the Social Security Administration recalculates the monthly benefit at full retirement age to credit the months that were withheld. The test does not apply to income from investments, pensions or retirement accounts — only to wages and self-employment income.

The taxable maximum. Social Security tax applies to earnings up to a cap, which rises to $192,300 in 2027 from $184,500. At 6.2% each for the employee and the employer, the most either pays in Social Security tax on wages is $11,922.60. The Medicare tax has no wage cap and is unchanged. The cap matters mainly to people still working at higher earnings in their late fifties and sixties; for everyone else, it changes nothing.

When the notice and the first payment arrive

Social Security sends each beneficiary a notice of the new amount. People with a my Social Security account can read it in the Message Center beginning in late November, ahead of the paper copy; the 2027 Medicare premium for people who have it withheld is expected to appear there at the same time. People who do not use online notices receive a one-page mailed notice in December. The first payment at the new amount comes with the January 2027 benefit. For most people, that is the Wednesday tied to their birthday: 13 January for birthdays on the 1st through the 10th, 20 January for the 11th through the 20th, and 27 January for the 21st through the 31st. SSI recipients are paid the increase on 31 December.

What’s worth checking now

  • The official 2027 COLA is 3.6%, announced 14 October 2026 by the Social Security Administration. Any earlier figure — including the 3.5%–3.6% estimates published in September — has been superseded.
  • A COLA percentage is not the same as what lands in an account. The 2027 Part B premium is announced separately and, for most people who have it withheld, comes out of the increase first — see the COLA page.
  • The earnings limit for people under full retirement age rose to $25,560, and to $67,920 in the year full retirement age is reached. The limit applies to wages and self-employment income only.
  • The taxable maximum rose to $192,300. It affects only earnings above the 2026 cap of $184,500.
  • The new benefit amount is sent to each beneficiary in a notice from the Social Security Administration; that notice, not a news estimate, is the number that governs the January payment.

The estimate season is over, and the useful thing about it ending is that the arguments about which forecaster was closest stop mattering. The percentage is set. What remains open is the part the Social Security Administration does not decide: what Medicare charges for 2027, and how much of the 3.6% survives the deduction. That number arrives from CMS, and until it does, the honest figure for the net raise is that there isn’t one yet.
