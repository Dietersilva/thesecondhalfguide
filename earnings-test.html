Working while you collect Social Security: the 2027 earnings test

Claim before full retirement age and keep working, and Social Security may hold back part of your benefit. The 2027 limits, what counts toward them, and what happens to the money.

By Edward Silva · 6 minute read · Checked against SSA’s 2027 announcement and independent explanations of the rule

The retirement earnings test is one of the most misunderstood rules in Social Security, mostly because its name sounds like a tax. It is not a tax and it is not a penalty. It applies to people who have started their benefits before full retirement age and who are still working, and it decides how much of the benefit is paid out while they do.

The 2027 limits were announced on 14 October, along with the cost-of-living adjustment. They rose, as they do every year. The rule itself is long-standing, and nothing about it changed this fall. What follows is the rule and this year’s numbers.

The earnings test, 2027
$25,560The annual earnings limit for anyone under full retirement age for the whole of 2027. It was $24,480 in 2026.
$1 for every $2Withheld from benefits for each dollar of earnings above that limit.
$67,920The limit in the calendar year a person reaches full retirement age. It was $65,160 in 2026.
$1 for every $3Withheld for each dollar above that limit, counting only earnings before the month full retirement age is reached.
$2,130 and $5,660The monthly versions of the two limits, used in the first year a person receives benefits.
After full retirement ageNo limit. The test stops applying in the month a person reaches full retirement age.
What countsWages and net earnings from self-employment. Pensions, annuities, interest and investment income do not.

Who it applies to

Only people who are receiving Social Security retirement benefits and have not yet reached full retirement age. For anyone born in 1960 or later, full retirement age is 67. Someone who is working but has not claimed is not subject to the test at all, because there is no benefit to withhold. The test is about the overlap: claiming early while still earning.

It also applies on top of the early-claiming reduction, not instead of it. A person who claims at 62 already receives a permanently smaller monthly amount. The earnings test then decides how much of that smaller amount is actually paid in a year of high earnings. The two are separate rules, and the reduction for claiming early is covered on the page about filing at 62.

The two rates, with numbers

In a year when a person is under full retirement age for the whole year, the limit is $25,560. Social Security withholds $1 of benefits for every $2 earned above it. A person who earns $35,560 is $10,000 over the limit, so $5,000 of benefits is withheld. A person who earns $25,000 is under it and nothing is withheld.

In the year a person reaches full retirement age, the test is milder in two ways. The limit is higher, at $67,920, and the rate is $1 for every $3 above it. Only earnings before the month of full retirement age count. A person who earns $76,920 before that month is $9,000 over, so $3,000 is withheld. Earnings after that month, however large, are ignored.

The test only ever looks at work. A large pension or a year of investment gains does not move it.

What counts, and what does not

Only earned income counts: gross wages from a job, and net earnings from self-employment. Pensions, annuities, interest, dividends and capital gains are not counted, and neither are investment returns generally. Rental income is not counted unless it is earned through self-employment. That is why two people with the same total income can face very different results. One has a salary, the other a pension and a brokerage account.

The test is also separate from income tax. Social Security benefits can be taxable whatever the earnings test does, and Medicare premiums can rise with income under a different rule, covered on the IRMAA page. Three different tests look at income and they do not share a definition.

The monthly rule in the first year

The annual limits would be hard on someone who works most of a year and then retires. So in the first year a person receives benefits, a monthly test applies as well. For 2027 the monthly limits are $2,130 for someone under full retirement age all year and $5,660 for the year full retirement age is reached, which are the annual limits divided by twelve. A month in which earnings stay under the monthly limit can produce a full benefit for that month, even if the person’s earnings for the whole year were above the annual limit.

An illustration. A person earns $80,000 from January through June, retires, and starts benefits in July. The annual test would flag the year. But from July through December, earnings are zero, which is under the monthly limit, so those months are paid in full. Self-employed people face an extra check, because Social Security also looks at whether they are still working substantially in their own business, whatever the income.

The money is held back, not lost

This is the part most descriptions skip. Benefits withheld under the earnings test are not forfeited. At full retirement age, Social Security recalculates the monthly benefit to credit the months in which payments were withheld, as if the person had claimed correspondingly later. A person with several withheld months ends up with a somewhat higher benefit for the rest of their life. How long it takes for the higher benefit to repay the amount withheld depends on the individual case. One published estimate puts it at roughly twelve to fifteen years, which is a rough figure and not an SSA number.

It is also why the test sits oddly beside the rule for waiting past full retirement age, covered on the delayed retirement credit page. That credit is a percentage added for each month of delay. The credit for withheld months is a recalculation that reflects months in which the person did not receive a benefit.

Where the limits come from

The earnings-test limits are not tied to the cost-of-living adjustment. They move with the national average wage index, which is why the limit rose 4.4% this year while the COLA was 3.6%. The wage figures behind that appear on the page on the 2027 COLA, alongside the new taxable maximum. Overpayments that arise when earnings were not reported on time are a separate subject, covered on the overpayment page.

One note on the news. The Claiming Age Clarity Act, which would rename ages 62 and 70 as “minimum” and “maximum” benefit ages, would change labels, not the earnings test. Its status is on its own page.

What’s worth keeping in mind

  • The 2027 limits are $25,560 for people under full retirement age all year ($1 withheld per $2 above) and $67,920 in the year full retirement age is reached ($1 per $3 above).
  • Only wages and net self-employment earnings count. Pensions, annuities, interest and investment income do not.
  • In the first year of benefits, a monthly test also applies: $2,130 under full retirement age, $5,660 in the year it is reached.
  • Withheld benefits are recalculated into a higher benefit at full retirement age. They are held back, not forfeited.
  • The test ends in the month a person reaches full retirement age.

The rule is simple to state and easy to get wrong in the details. That is why the numbers above are worth having written down before a job offer, a part-time contract, or a first benefit payment arrives.
