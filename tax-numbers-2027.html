The 2027 tax numbers: the standard deduction, the brackets and the 65-plus add-on

The IRS has set next year’s inflation-adjusted amounts. The standard deduction rises $1,000 for couples, the senior deduction does not move, and none of it touches the return you file this spring.

By Edward Silva · 6 minute read · Checked against the IRS announcement and independent write-ups that cite it

On 5 November the IRS published the inflation adjustments for tax year 2027 in Revenue Procedure 2026-35, with a companion news release. The first thing to get straight is what they apply to. These are the amounts for income earned in 2027, on returns filed in 2028. The return due in April is a 2026 return, and it uses the 2026 numbers, which are on the standard deduction page.

Most of the announcement is arithmetic. The seven rates stay where they are, and the dollar thresholds move up by about 3.2%. Here is what that does, and what it does not do for readers 65 and older.

The 2027 tax numbers, in dollars
$33,200Standard deduction, married filing jointly. Up $1,000 from 2026.
$16,600Standard deduction, single or married filing separately. Up $500.
$24,925Standard deduction, head of household. Up $775.
$1,700 or $2,100The extra standard deduction for being 65 or older (or blind): $1,700 for each qualifying spouse if married, $2,100 if single or head of household. Up $50 each, as reported.
$6,000The senior deduction for people 65 and older, for 2025 through 2028. Set in law, not indexed, and it phases out above $75,000 of income single or $150,000 joint.
$51,050 / $102,100The top of the 0% long-term capital gains rate, single and joint, as reported.

The standard deduction

For 2027 the base standard deduction is $33,200 for married couples filing jointly, $16,600 for single filers and married people filing separately, and $24,925 for heads of household. The last figure was projected at $24,950 by some forecasters, and the IRS number is $24,925.

The age add-on is the part specific to readers of this site. An extra amount is added to the standard deduction for a taxpayer who is 65 or older, and again for one who is blind. Reporting on the revenue procedure gives $1,700 per qualifying condition for married filers and $2,100 for unmarried filers who are not surviving spouses, each $50 more than in 2026. Separately, the $6,000 senior deduction created in 2025 sits outside the standard deduction and does not change in 2027. It runs through 2028 and phases out above $75,000 of income for a single filer or $150,000 for a joint return. Both are on the senior deduction page, and the phase-out is on its own page.

Put together, using 2027 amounts: a single filer who is 66 with income under the senior-deduction phase-out line has $16,600 plus $2,100 plus $6,000, which is $24,700 of income that is not taxed. A married couple who are both 67 and are under the joint line have $33,200 plus $1,700 twice plus $6,000 twice, which is $48,600. Both sums are arithmetic on the published amounts, not a statement about anyone’s tax bill.

The brackets moved about 3.2%. The $6,000 senior deduction did not move at all. It is set in law, not indexed.

The brackets

The rates are 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35% and 37%. The thresholds, reported from the revenue procedure, are below. Each figure is the taxable income above which a rate begins.

Where each rate begins, 2027 (taxable income above)
12%$12,800 single. $25,600 married filing jointly.
22%$52,025 single. $104,050 married filing jointly.
24%$109,125 single. $218,250 married filing jointly.
37%$661,375 single. $793,650 married filing jointly.

Those are taxable income, which is what is left after the deductions above. A couple whose income is a pension and Social Security, for example, may land well inside the 12% bracket after the standard and senior deductions, which is why the deduction amounts matter more to many readers than the bracket edges. How benefits are counted is on the Social Security taxation page, and the thresholds that decide how much of a benefit is taxable are not indexed at all.

The capital gains line

Long-term capital gains have their own rates of 0%, 15% and 20%. For 2027 the 0% rate is reported to apply to taxable income up to $51,050 for a single filer and $102,100 for a joint return. That is the number that comes up for people selling a house or taking gains in the years between work and required distributions, and the reporting on it is consistent across several tax sites, though this page did not read the revenue procedure’s table directly.

What this does not change

It does not change the 2026 return, the senior deduction’s dollar amount or its phase-out, the amounts that make Social Security taxable, or Medicare premiums. The Part B premium and the income thresholds for the Medicare surcharge, which look back two years, are announced separately, and as of this writing they have not been. The surcharge is on the IRMAA page. Required withdrawals, which push income up in the early seventies, are on the RMD page.

The retirement-account limits that the IRS announced a week later, on 12 November, are on their own page.

What’s worth keeping in mind

  • The IRS announced the 2027 amounts on 5 November 2026. They apply to income earned in 2027, on returns filed in 2028, not to the return due this spring.
  • Standard deduction: $33,200 joint, $16,600 single, $24,925 head of household.
  • The age-65 add-on is reported at $1,700 per qualifying spouse if married and $2,100 if unmarried. The $6,000 senior deduction is unchanged.
  • The seven rates are unchanged; thresholds rose about 3.2%. The 0% capital gains rate reaches $51,050 single and $102,100 joint, as reported.
  • The Medicare Part B premium and surcharge thresholds for 2027 have not been announced.

The announcement is a set of numbers for next year. What a particular household owes depends on its income and its other deductions, and this page leaves that to the forms.
