The 2 August 2027 total solar eclipse: where it crosses, how long it lasts

Southern Spain gets a few minutes of darkness in the late morning. Egypt gets more than six. For a reader in the lower 48, the next total eclipse at home is in 2044.

By Edward Silva · 6 minute read · Checked against Spain’s IGN, NASA and eclipse-guide reporting

A total solar eclipse is the rare travel event with a fixed date, a fixed place and no second chance. The next one that Americans can reasonably plan around is on 2 August 2027. It crosses the Atlantic, the Strait of Gibraltar, southern Spain and North Africa, then continues across Egypt and the Arabian Peninsula to Somalia. Tour operators are already selling trips, which is the reason this page is worth having nine months ahead of the date and not nine days.

It is also a page about a calendar, not a recommendation. What follows is the geometry, the durations and the logistics reported so far, and what the sources do not agree on.

The 2 August 2027 eclipse, in numbers
2 August 2027The date of the total solar eclipse.
About 6 minutes 23 secondsThe longest totality, near Luxor in Egypt’s Nile Valley.
About 4 minutes 48 secondsTotality at Ceuta, the longest in Spanish territory, per Spain’s National Geographic Institute (IGN).
About 2 minutes 54 secondsTotality at Cádiz, per IGN. Another guide gives 2 minutes 56 seconds.
About 1 minute 48 secondsTotality at Málaga, per IGN.
About 10:45Local time totality begins along the Spanish coast, in the morning.
23 August 2044The next total eclipse visible from the contiguous United States, per NASA.

Where the path goes

In Spain, totality covers the autonomous cities of Ceuta and Melilla, almost all of Cádiz province, much of Málaga province, and the southernmost parts of Granada and Almería. Gibraltar is in the path. The reason these numbers differ so much within a few hundred miles is geometry: the shadow’s center line crosses the coast at a different angle than its edges, and a place near the edge gets a shorter eclipse than a place near the middle. Standing in the wrong town can cost minutes.

Egypt is the other side of that trade. The longest totality on land is near Luxor, at about six minutes and twenty-three seconds. Cairo and the pyramids at Giza are not on the path. They would see a deep partial eclipse of about 95%, which is dramatic and is not totality. A traveler who books a trip around Cairo to see the eclipse has booked the wrong city.

Ninety-five percent of the Sun covered is a good afternoon. One hundred percent is a different experience, and it begins and ends at the edge of the path.

Spain or Egypt: what the reporting says

The guides agree on the trade. Egypt offers longer totality and much lower risk of cloud in August. Spain is easier to reach and to move around in, but the Atlantic coast has a higher chance of cloud, and a narrow strip of the south coast is expected to be crowded. One guide expects heavy road congestion and says parking will be a significant problem. A few minutes of totality is a short window to leave to chance at the end of a traffic jam.

Reported durations differ by a few seconds between sources, which is normal for a path-edge calculation and not an error. They are close enough to compare cities, and not close enough to plan to the second. One guide claims a mainland Spanish town gets nearly the maximum Spanish duration, which disagrees with the IGN figures above, so this page does not repeat it.

The part about eyes

Looking at the Sun is dangerous except for one moment. The widely repeated guidance in the eclipse guides is that eclipse glasses meeting the ISO 12312-2 standard must be worn during every partial phase, before and after totality, and may be removed only during the brief total phase, inside the path of totality, when no bright part of the Sun remains visible, and put back on as soon as the first bright sliver returns. Ordinary sunglasses are not a substitute, and eclipse glasses do not make a camera, binoculars or a telescope safe: those need their own solar filters fitted to the front. Checking the marking on the glasses, and not the packaging, is the point. The agency guidance (NASA, the American Astronomical Society) is worth reading directly before the date, because most of the pages that repeat it are selling glasses.

Why 2044 matters to a reader in their sixties

The last total eclipse across the lower 48 was on 8 April 2024. According to NASA, the next one that can be seen from the contiguous United States is on 23 August 2044, with a path through the northern Plains and the Rockies. Alaska gets a total eclipse in March 2033. Someone who is 62 today will be 80 in 2044. That is not a reason to book anything. It is a fact about the calendar that the travel pages tend to leave out, and it explains why a trip to Spain or Egypt in 2027 is being treated as the one that counts.

What to settle early

The items that cost money to get wrong are the ones that are fixed in time. The date is fixed, which is why tour operators have been offering itineraries for some time (for example, an Egypt program in late July and early August 2027), and why a booking made late has fewer choices. Travel insurance matters more for a trip with a one-day purpose, and the travel insurance page covers what the policies actually do. Entry rules are a separate matter: Spain is part of the Schengen area, and the EU’s new authorization is covered on the ETIAS page. Egypt’s entry rules were not checked for this page and should be confirmed with the State Department before booking.

What’s worth keeping in mind

  • The total solar eclipse is on 2 August 2027, crossing southern Spain and North Africa, then continuing across Egypt and the Arabian Peninsula to Somalia.
  • Longest totality: about 6 minutes 23 seconds near Luxor. In Spain: about 4 minutes 48 seconds at Ceuta, 2 minutes 54 seconds at Cádiz, 1 minute 48 seconds at Málaga.
  • Cairo and Giza are outside the path: about 95% partial, not total.
  • Wear ISO 12312-2 eclipse glasses for every partial phase; remove them only during totality, inside the path.
  • The next total eclipse visible from the contiguous United States is 23 August 2044.

The eclipse will happen on schedule. Everything else about getting there is the part that can go wrong, and most of it is decided months before.
