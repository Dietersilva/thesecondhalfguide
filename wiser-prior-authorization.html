Prior authorization has reached traditional Medicare in six states

A CMS pilot that began in January requires approval before certain Part B services in Arizona, New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, Texas and Washington. Medicare Advantage is not part of it.

By Edward Silva · 6 minute read · Checked against CRS, KFF and independent reporting on the CMS model

One of the standard arguments for choosing Original Medicare over an Advantage plan is that Original Medicare does not make you ask permission first. In six states that has been less true since January. The pilot is called WISeR, short for Wasteful and Inappropriate Service Reduction, and it brings prior authorization into traditional Medicare for a list of Part B services.

This is not new this week. It began in January 2026, and it runs for six years. It is worth a page during Annual Enrollment because the choice between Original Medicare and an Advantage plan is being made now, and one of the usual talking points has an exception.

What it is

Prior authorization means a provider has to get approval before delivering a service, or Medicare may not pay for it. Advantage plans have used it for years. Original Medicare mostly has not, with exceptions such as some equipment, covered on the equipment prior authorization page. WISeR extends the idea to a set of Part B services for people living in the six states, and it is a CMS Innovation Center model, which means a time-limited test and not a permanent rule.

It applies to people in Original Medicare who live in those states. It does not apply to Medicare Advantage members, who are already subject to their own plans’ rules. Whether it will be extended to other states or services is not announced. KFF notes CMS could expand it, and no expansion for January 2027 turned up in the reporting checked here.

Which services

The list is a set of Part B services chosen as vulnerable to overuse. KFF’s description names skin substitutes used in treating severe or chronic wounds, electrical nerve stimulator implants and certain orthopedic pain services such as cervical fusion and epidural steroid injections. Other summaries include arthroscopy for knee osteoarthritis. Sources count the categories differently, from about 13 to 17, so this page does not give a single number. The authoritative list is the provider guide CMS publishes, and it can change during the model.

The rule is not that everything in Original Medicare now needs approval. It is that a specific list does, in six specific states.

Who decides, and how the decision-makers are paid

Each state has one participating technology company that reviews requests. Reporting says the companies use artificial intelligence to help with review and that licensed clinicians make every decision not to approve a request. Vendors are not paid a flat fee. CMS describes a performance-based share of savings, and the American Hospital Association puts it at 10% to 20% of the savings associated with denied care. Critics point to that as an incentive to deny, and supporters, such as America’s Physician Groups, say the model could reduce costs that fall on taxpayers and beneficiaries. Both positions are on the record. The page does not take one.

Providers submit the requests, not patients. A patient’s practical contact with the model is often indirect: a scheduled procedure waiting for an approval, or a denial that the provider has to pursue. How a decision not to approve can be challenged was not settled in the reporting reviewed, and the American Hospital Association has asked CMS to let patients appeal. The page leaves the process out for that reason.

What is contested

The model has drawn criticism from advocacy groups and some members of Congress. Two representatives introduced the Seniors Deserve SMARTER Care Act, which would repeal it. No vote on that bill turned up. The Center for Medicare Advocacy has called early reports on the model troubling, which is the view of an advocacy group and not a measured finding. The model’s own results are not in yet.

Where this fits in the enrollment choice

None of this is a recommendation. It is one fact that belongs next to the others in the choice covered on the Advantage or Original page: Advantage plans use prior authorization broadly, Original Medicare uses it for a defined list, and in six states that list is longer than it used to be. The window for changing a plan is covered on the open enrollment page, and the services that fall outside both are on the gaps page.

What’s worth keeping in mind

  • Since January 2026, a pilot called WISeR has required prior authorization for a list of Part B services in Arizona, New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, Texas and Washington.
  • It applies to Original Medicare only; Medicare Advantage plans are outside it. It is scheduled to run to 31 December 2031.
  • Services reported include skin substitutes, nerve stimulator implants and some orthopedic pain procedures. Counts differ from 13 to 17 by source.
  • Providers submit the requests. Vendors are paid a share of savings, which is both the model’s design and the main criticism.
  • The model is a time-limited test. A bill to repeal it has been introduced; no vote was found.

The accurate statement is narrower than either side of the argument makes it. Original Medicare still does not require approval for most services. In six states, for a defined list, it now does.
