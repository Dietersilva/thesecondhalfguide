The Medicare Prescription Payment Plan: same cap, paid monthly

The plan does not lower what Part D costs. It spreads the out-of-pocket cap — $2,400 in 2027 — into monthly bills. And changing plans in open enrollment ends it.

By Edward Silva · 5 minute read · Checked against CMS’s 2027 announcement and independent plan and advocacy sources

Part D has an annual cap on what a person pays out of pocket for covered drugs: $2,100 in 2026, rising to $2,400 in 2027. The cap protects the total. It does not protect the timing. A person who fills an expensive drug in January can owe a large share of the year’s cap at the pharmacy counter in a single visit.

The Medicare Prescription Payment Plan exists for that problem. It does nothing about the total. It changes when the money is due.

The plan, at a glance
2027 cap$2,400 in covered Part D drug costs per year, whether or not you use the plan. The 2026 cap is $2,100.
2027 deductibleThe standard deductible rises to $700 from $615. That is the most a plan may charge; some plans charge less.
What it changesThe timing of payments. Instead of paying at the pharmacy, you are billed monthly by your plan.
Cost to joinNothing. There is no fee or interest for using it.
How to joinThrough your own Part D plan. It is voluntary, and it can be started at any point in the year, not only during open enrollment.
Switching plansParticipation is with a specific plan. A person who changes plans needs to enroll with the new plan.

How the monthly bill is calculated

Once enrolled, a person stops paying at the counter for covered drugs. The plan bills them each month instead. The amount is based on what the prescriptions cost, plus any balance carried from the previous month, divided by the number of months left in the year.

An example, using the 2027 deductible. In January a person fills a prescription that costs $700. Without the plan they would pay $700 at the pharmacy. With it they pay nothing at the counter, and the plan bills about $58 for January, which is $700 divided by the twelve months left in the year. In February they fill another prescription for $120. The plan adds it to the remaining balance and divides again by the eleven months left. By December the balance is paid off. The arithmetic is the plan’s formula; what it does is smooth the same total into smaller pieces.

The plan changes when the money is due, not how much of it there is.

The worst case, in dollars

Take the largest possible case for 2027: a person whose first fill of the year is expensive enough to reach the whole $2,400 cap at once. Without the plan, that is $2,400 due at the pharmacy. With the plan, the January bill is $2,400 divided by twelve, which is $200. The cap is the same and so is the total. The plan only changes how it arrives: one bill of $2,400, or a bill of $200 a month for twelve months. For a smaller January fill, the monthly figure shrinks in proportion.

How a person actually asks

Enrollment goes through the person’s own Part D plan, not through Medicare directly. The plan, in turn, tells the pharmacy that the person is enrolled, so the pharmacy bills the plan instead of collecting at the counter. Each plan has its own phone line and process for the request, and the plan’s member services is where to start. The program is voluntary, and a person can also leave it at any time. The details of the request vary by plan, so the plan’s own instructions are the ones that apply.

What it does not do

It does not lower a drug’s price. It does not change the cap, so a person who would reach $2,400 anyway reaches it with or without the plan. It does not change which drugs a plan covers or what tier they sit on. And the monthly bills are not optional once a person is enrolled: they are what the person owes. According to advocacy-group descriptions of the rules, a plan can end a person’s participation if the bills go unpaid, though the drug coverage itself continues, and returning to the plan means first settling what is owed.

For a person who would pay most of the cap across the year anyway through a steady set of monthly prescriptions, the difference is small. The plan matters most when one or two fills early in the year are large. That is a statement about how the formula behaves, not advice about who should use it.

Why it comes up in October

Annual Enrollment runs from 15 October through 7 December, and it is when many people change Part D plans. The plan’s participation does not follow the person: under the rules described for 2026, people already in the plan are carried forward when they stay in the same plan, and people who move to a different plan need to enroll with the new one. Coverage chosen in the window starts on 1 January 2027, so a person who joins a new plan and wants to spread January’s costs has to ask the new plan to enroll them.

Two other 2027 Part D facts sit alongside it in coverage of next year’s changes: insulin stays capped at $35 a month for a covered supply, and adult vaccines recommended by the federal advisory committee remain free under Part D. Neither depends on the payment plan.

The rest of the 2027 Part D picture is covered on the page on 2027 Medicare costs and on the page about the drug cap. The plan is only a payment mechanism sitting on top of those numbers.

What’s worth checking

  • The 2027 out-of-pocket cap is $2,400 for everyone with Part D, with or without the Prescription Payment Plan.
  • The plan costs nothing to join and can be started at any time through your own Part D plan.
  • The monthly bill is calculated from your drug costs plus any prior balance, divided by the months left in the year.
  • Participation is tied to a plan. If you switch plans during open enrollment (15 October–7 December), you need to enroll with the new plan.
  • The plan does not reduce the total. A person who would pay $2,400 pays $2,400, only in smaller pieces.

The plan is one of the least dramatic features Medicare has added to Part D, and for a certain kind of bill it is the difference between a manageable January and an unmanageable one. It is worth understanding precisely because it is so easily mistaken for a discount.
